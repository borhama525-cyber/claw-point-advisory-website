What the Stack Method is

The Stack Method is a creative-finance structure that combines a note the seller carries back with a loan from a primary lender. Together, those two sources form the full capital stack: enough to cover the purchase price and the closing costs, and sometimes more. The name comes from stacking more than one funding source on a single deal.

You may also hear it called the Morby Method. The idea is not new: piggyback loans, 80-20 financing and purchase-money mortgages all use the same principle, and the structure seems to pick up a new name every decade or so.

Done properly, it lets a buyer close with little or no cash of their own, and in some deals walk away from the closing table with money.

Who it’s for

The typical Stack buyer is an investor who wants the property but would rather not lock a large down payment into it. You might be building a portfolio and prefer to spread your capital across several purchases instead of one. Or you have found a motivated seller who is open to carrying part of the price, but real cash still has to be at the table on closing day. When the seller is willing to stay invested and the numbers hold up, this structure turns “I can’t cover the down payment” into a closing date.

The three pieces of the stack

Each funding source in a Stack deal does one job.

  1. 01
    The primary loan. A lender funds the largest share of the price in first position. If you don’t have a primary lender yet, we can provide one.
  2. 02
    The seller’s reinvestment. The seller covers part of the rest, most often by carrying back a second-position note, or occasionally through a preferred equity position. Either way, the seller stays invested in the property instead of taking all their equity in cash, and that is what makes the structure work.
  3. 03
    Short-term cash to close. Even with both loans agreed on paper, cash still has to arrive at the closing table. That is where we come in: we wire the cash to close into escrow, the deal records, and the seller’s reinvestment repays us.

How the money moves

A Stack deal is one purchase that settles in two steps.

The primary closing brings the money in. The primary lender’s funds and our short-term cash land at title. The seller is paid, the deed records and you own the property.

The secondary closing settles it. Think of it as an immediate refinance of our short-term funds into the seller’s note. The seller carry is documented and the held funds are disbursed: the seller’s proceeds, our repayment, the closing fees and any cash back the structure allows.

Everything depends on the escrow disbursement instructions. Our funds are held at title and released when the deal records, and we agree those instructions with the title company before any money moves. That is what lets short-term funds go out and come back cleanly.

The one test every Stack deal must pass

One check separates a fundable Stack deal from a hopeful one: the seller carry has to be large enough to repay our funding, our fee and the second-closing costs. If it is, the deal works. The calculator sizes our funding to what the carry supports and shows exactly what lands on you at the table, either cash to bring or cash back. If the carry can’t cover it, it isn’t a Stack deal we can fund. Restructure the carry, adjust the price, or bring the difference at the first closing. This rule is what keeps everyone in the deal protected, the seller included.

Doing it properly: disclosure and paperwork

The Stack Method sometimes gets a bad name from deals done carelessly. Done properly, it is straightforward:

  • The primary lender sees the whole structure. The seller carry and the source of the cash to close are disclosed to your primary lender, and we confirm the lender’s seasoning and sourcing rules before funding, not after. If a lender’s guidelines can’t accommodate the structure, the answer is a different lender.
  • The seller carry has its own paperwork. It is documented in its own note and addendum, not buried in the purchase contract, so your primary lender can underwrite it cleanly.
  • Everything settles through licensed title and escrow. Two settlement statements, written disbursement instructions, and funds that never pass through personal accounts.
  • Government-backed primary loans don’t fit. FHA and VA loans have strict rules on secondary financing, so Stack deals use investor financing from a private or investor-focused lender. We can provide one if you need it.

When it isn’t a Stack deal

  • The seller wants every dollar at closing. No seller reinvestment means no Stack. If there is a wholesaler’s spread in the deal, look at the Echo Method instead: the same down-payment funding, repaid from the spread rather than the carry.
  • The carry is too small to repay the funding. See the test above. Restructure the deal or bring cash.
  • The primary lender requires seasoned funds. Some lenders won’t accept short-term funds for the cash to close. We check this at the start, because it is one of the first questions to answer.
  • The numbers only work at an inflated price. If the deal only works on an unrealistic value, it doesn’t work.

Common questions about the Stack Method

Is the Stack Method legal?

Combining a primary loan with seller financing is a long-established structure when it is fully disclosed: the seller carry is documented, the primary lender underwrites with the whole structure in view, and everything settles through licensed title and escrow. That is the only way we fund it. Rules vary by state and lender, so have your own real estate attorney review your deal.

Do I need my own cash?

Often very little or none. Whether you bring cash, bring nothing or leave with cash depends on one thing: how the seller carry compares with the funding it has to repay. The calculator shows which side of that line your deal is on.

What if the seller carry is too small?

Then the structure doesn’t fully fund itself. You can negotiate a larger carry, lower the price, or bring the shortfall to the first closing. Your written terms show the exact figure.

Stack or Echo: which is my deal?

Both cover a down payment short-term. The difference is what repays it. In a Stack, the seller’s note repays us, which suits buyers keeping the property. In an Echo, the spread on a double close repays us, which suits wholesale deals where everyone cashes out. Seller staying in? Stack. Everyone cashing out? Echo.

What does the seller get out of it?

Most of their price in cash at closing, plus a note that pays them over time, often a better overall result than cutting the price. The note is secured by a property they know well, and the escrow instructions that repay us protect them too.

How fast does it move?

Your closing timeline is set mainly by your primary lender and title company. We review every request individually and respond as soon as we have what we need.

What does it cost?

Each deal is priced individually based on its structure, timeline and risk. The fee fields in the calculator are editable estimates. Your written terms show the actual cost.

Why do I need a transaction coordinator?

A Stack closing happens in two steps, and the paperwork connecting them decides everything. An experienced coordinator keeps the addendum, the disbursement instructions and both settlement statements aligned. If you don’t have one, we can introduce you to someone who has done these before.

Don’t have a primary lender? We can provide one.

Many deals need a lender for the largest share of the price. Tick “I need a primary lender” in your request and we’ll bring one into your deal.
